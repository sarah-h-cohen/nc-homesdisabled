Staffing overview

There are a LOT of errors in the staffing data. In many cases, they change by a factor of 10 periodically for no reason . It affects more than 10 percent of the reports.

Sometimes the number of staff seems to be off by a factor of 10 – either 100th of the real value, or 100x the real value. But sometimes the number is just crazy off for no apparent reason. (Example, total staff will quietly sit at 20 for all the other years, but be 7 for one year)

The errors more often hit the larger facilties, particularly government ones.

This is an attempt to fix them where I can and remove them where I can’t.

One difference from others is that I’ll also look at total staff, not direct just direct care. The reason is I can see at times that this isn’t right – they’ll have nursing staff and not direct care, for example. But it HAS to be higher. A credibly minimim of a 6-person place where they are there all the time is 21 shifts of direct care and 7 shifts of custodial, and possibly 1/2 position of adminsitrative. That comes out to just about 7 people per place. (5.25 direct care, 1.75 other)

The key values:

  • Direct care staff vs Total staff : The rules and the important values are direct care staff, so we’ll use them when we can. However, these can fluctuate a lot, and seem to be in error more than the total staff, so we will check on how much bigger total staff is.

Stanford paper criteria

A paper from a Stanford researcher evaluates quality of care and ownership patterns. Its appendix goes through the criteria they used to determine whether there was an error in the staffing values.

This analysis always uses direct care staff, not total staff; it also uses a resident estimate, not the licensed beds. The Medicaid rules are all about direct care staff. The resident estimate is used because they said the bed count is sometimes inappropriately including other facilties that are owned by the same people. In both of these cases, I’ve seen reasons to consider the total staff and the icf bed count as backup measures.

In short the criteria for removing a row was:

  1. According to the rules, there must be at least one direct care staff on premises at all times. Some group homes send their clients to daytime activities during the week, so they estimated there must be at least 2 direct care staff to cover the rest of the time.

  2. For an ICF made up entirely of the least severely disabled adults, the ratio is 1 FTE per 6.4 residents. That cutoff is .3125.

  3. The most stringent ratio – the most profoundly disabled children – the legal ratio is 1.3125 per resident. They multiplied by 3 to take account of places that voluntarily overstaffed. (Note to self: This failed in places that have falling resident counts.)

  4. They dropped surveys in which the value was way off from the median for the facility (< .5 or >2), but was checked against a possible spike or drop in the number of residents.

Adjustments to Stanford paper

  • Drop facilities that closed before 2020, and those with less than 5 years’ history.

  • Check for total staff of at least 3 before counting it as too low.

  • Check to see if multiplying by 100 will get you to the median value of the others surveys from this facility. There are a handful where every year is off by a lot, but only a few. (Compute the median only of those with at least 2 total staff, not including the one that’s being evaluated )

  • Check the large ratios against beds, not residents, before dropping. If that ends up reasonable, keep it. That will take care of the ones that are falling fast.

create_staff_bitmap <- function ( dc, tot, dc_res_ratio, tot_res_ratio, dc_bed_ratio ) {

  bitmap <- "111111"

  if ( dc < 2 )  str_sub( bitmap, 1, 1) <-  "0"
  if ( tot < 3 )  str_sub( bitmap, 2, 1) <-  "0"
  if ( dc_res_ratio  < .3 )  str_sub( bitmap, 3, 1) <-  "0"
  if ( tot_res_ratio < .5 )  str_sub( bitmap, 4, 1) <-  "0"
  if ( dc_res_ratio > 3.5 )  str_sub( bitmap, 5, 1) <-  "0"
  if ( dc_bed_ratio > 3.5 )  str_sub ( bitmap, 6, 1) <- "0"

  return ( bitmap)

}
